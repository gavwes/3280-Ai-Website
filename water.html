Water & Communities

AI is thirsty, and it's drinking from local water supplies.

Data centers use huge amounts of fresh water to keep their chips cool. Many are built in places already facing drought, and the communities next door often see few of the benefits.

How it works

Why computers need water

AI chips run extremely hot. Many data centers cool them with evaporative cooling: water absorbs the heat and evaporates into the air through cooling towers, the same way sweat cools your body. Water that evaporates is gone from the local supply.

Data centers usually need clean, fresh water, often the same drinking-quality water that homes use, because minerals and bacteria can damage equipment.

There is also an indirect water cost: many power plants that generate the electricity for data centers also use water for cooling.

Location matters

Built where water is already scarce

Companies pick data center sites for cheap land, cheap electricity, tax breaks and fast internet connections. Water availability isn't always the top concern, so many data centers end up in hot, dry regions where every gallon counts.

Hot climates

Hotter air means cooling systems work harder and evaporate more water, especially in summer when supplies are lowest.

Drought regions

Places like the American Southwest have seen rapid data center growth while facing long-term drought and shrinking rivers.

Shared aquifers

Some data centers pump groundwater from the same underground sources that farms and households rely on.

Little transparency

Water use is often treated as a trade secret, so residents may not know how much their local data center uses.

Who pays the cost

The benefits are global. The costs are local.

Anyone in the world can use an AI tool, but the electricity demand, water use, noise and land use land on the specific community where the data center is built.

  • Few long-term jobs. Data centers create many construction jobs, but once built they often employ only a few dozen people.
  • Tax breaks. States and towns often offer large tax incentives to attract data centers, which can reduce money for schools and services.
  • Higher utility costs. New power plants and transmission lines built for data centers can raise electricity bills for everyone on the grid.
  • Noise and land. Neighbors have complained about constant humming from cooling equipment and the loss of farmland and open space.
  • Unequal power. Rural and lower-income communities often have less say in these deals than large tech companies do.

Case study: The Dalles, Oregon. After a legal fight over public records, the city revealed in 2022 that Google's data centers used more than a quarter of the city's water. Residents learned how much water was going to the facilities only after the local newspaper sued to make it public.

Closer to home: South Carolina. Google operates a large data center in Berkeley County, and its request to pump more groundwater from a local aquifer drew public pushback from residents and conservation groups. With more data centers being proposed across the state, these questions are coming to our own backyard.
